There's one piece of design that never ships in Figma: how a person feels in the first five minutes after walking through a door. At Friends of Figma Barcelona, that one is mine.

## the community

**Friends of Figma Barcelona** is Figma's official chapter in the city: an open community of designers and product people who gather several times a year for talks, panels and watch parties. It isn't a paid conference or a closed club — it's people showing up after work, often alone, to see whether they fit.

Every event rests on a team of volunteers. I'm part of that **staff**.

## from attendee to staff

I arrived at this community as an **attendee**. I went to my first event knowing nobody, with that awkward mix of excitement and self-consciousness anyone who has gone somewhere alone will recognise. I still remember exactly who came over to talk to me, and how much the night changed from that point on.

I came back. And again. Until I stopped being the one arriving and became **part of the staff**: the one who now walks over.

That path is precisely what makes me useful in the role. I know exactly where people who come alone end up standing, at what moment they start thinking about leaving early, and which sentence makes them stay.

## the moment of connection

When the doors open, the job changes. I leave the logistics behind and take the spot where I'm needed most: **the entrance**.

My job isn't handing out badges — that's the excuse. It's reading the room: spotting whoever arrived alone, whoever has spent ten minutes looking at their phone in a corner, whoever is standing at the edge of a group. And making the introduction that person won't make on their own.

> Connecting a first-timer with someone who has been in the community for years is, in practice, a design problem: reducing the friction of a first contact.

It's a role you do with names, memory and a question ready. It works when, half an hour later, that person is already in a conversation that doesn't need me.

## what i take away

- **The welcome is an interface.** A person's first five minutes at an event can be designed like an onboarding: fewer steps, fewer doubts, one clear action.
- **Operations are invisible when they're done well.** Nobody comments that check-in was smooth; they only comment when it wasn't. That's the standard.
- **Connecting people is a skill, not a trait.** You train it: remembering names, listening two sentences longer than is comfortable, and knowing when to step out of the conversation.
- **Having been the one who arrived knowing nobody** is what lets me spot that person now.
- **Community gives back more than you put in.** Volunteering on a Saturday morning assembling lanyards has given me the strongest professional network I have in Barcelona.
